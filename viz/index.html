A learner-first guide to an unconditional, Lean-checked construction

Can a finitely instructed robot avoid every straight line forever? Yes.

Erdős Problem 193 asks whether every infinite walk on the 3D integer grid, using only finitely many step types, must eventually visit three points on one line. This construction gives a counterexample.

I came to the problem as an engineer and math hobbyist. The finished formulas were not a useful starting point for me: I first needed to know what every object was doing, why the walk must be infinite, and why a large computation could never settle “forever.” This site now explains the proof in that order.

The sentence I can now say from memory

Trace a Hilbert route, select regularly spaced same-state checkpoints, then lift each checkpoint by using its route index as height.

Bounded gaps give a finite menu of 3D steps. A two-adic law shared by every selected pair then turns the assumption of three collinear points into the impossible claim that the sum of two odd numbers is odd.

1. Trace

Use the discrete Hilbert path as an infinite route through the 2D grid.

2. Select

Keep one index from each 16-index block, always with one terminal orientation state.

3. Lift

Turn a selected planar point H(n) into the 3D point (H(n), n).

4. Bound

Successive selected indices differ by 4 through 28, so only finitely many 3D steps are possible.

5. Fingerprint

Measure a planar chord by the common powers of two in its coordinates and one parity bit.

6. Contradict

The adjacent pairs make two coprime scale factors odd; the endpoint pair says their sum is odd.

Where I got stuck

The proof becomes much easier when four similar-sounding ideas are kept separate.

Finite menu is not a finite walk

The robot walks forever. Only the collection of allowed displacement vectors must be finite.

Select before lift

We do not lift every Hilbert point and hope. We first choose a controlled subsequence, then use each selected index as height.

Sixteen suffixes are not sixteen states

A two-digit base-4 suffix has 16 possibilities, but the Hilbert decoder carries only four terminal orientation states.

The selector does not search for blue

Each block uses one fixed state-to-offset lookup. The chosen suffix cancels the incoming state and guarantees the target state.

The fingerprint is deliberately incomplete

It forgets length and direction. It retains exactly the binary scale and parity pattern needed by the pair law.

Lean and finite checks do different jobs

The 500,000-step artifact tests an implementation. The Lean theorem checks the general infinite argument.

Why the robot keeps walking

One selected point appears in every 16-index block.

The selector chooses one of four offsets—1, 3, 5, or 13—according to the block’s incoming terminal state. Consecutive chosen indices are therefore 4 through 28 Hilbert steps apart.

Hilbert neighbors touch on the floor. A bounded number of floor steps plus a bounded height change places every realized 3D move inside one fixed finite box.

Why three points never line up

The same first digit mismatch controls two different objects.

For two selected indices, the first differing base-4 digit determines both the power of two in their index gap and a fingerprint of their planar chord. This is the Hilbert pair law.

On a supposed straight line, integer gaps scale once while both planar coordinates scale together. Their binary fingerprints cannot satisfy all three pair laws.

Proof, computation, and review

“Checked for a long time” and “proved forever” are not interchangeable.

The saved artifact contains 500,001 exact vertices, and an independent exhaustive check found no collinear triple in that finite prefix. Those results are valuable implementation evidence, but no finite prefix proves an infinite statement.

The Lean development instead checks a theorem covering every natural-number position and every ordered triple of distinct selected vertices. It builds without unfinished placeholders or project-specific axioms.

The remaining boundary

A kernel-checked formal proof can still encode an unintended statement. External mathematical review and community acceptance are pending, so the repository exposes the manuscript, definitions, axiom audit, artifacts, and failed routes for inspection.

How this work was developed

Human-steered, AI-assisted mathematics—and a record of the questions that changed the proof.

Erik Kalviainen used Paseo, Oh My Pi, and GPT-5.6 as a research stack. Erik chose the problem, challenged unclear claims, requested exact counterexamples, and kept redirecting the work toward an unconditional proof and formal verification. GPT-5.6 performed much of the algebra, coding, adversarial testing, writing, and Lean development.

The first idea—lifting every Hilbert point—fails at indices 3, 4, and 5. That failure was not hidden; it forced the eventual selector. The guided proof preserves similar moments of confusion because they expose which distinctions actually carry the argument.
