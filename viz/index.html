Erdős Problem 193

Can a robot walk forever with a finite menu of moves—and never visit three points on one line? Yes.

Erdős Problem 193 asks whether every such walk on the 3D integer grid must eventually contain three collinear points. This construction gives an explicit counterexample.

1
Build the walk

Trace. Select. Lift.

Let \(H(n)\) be the point reached at index \(n\) on an infinite discrete Hilbert curve. The same recursive rule builds routes on larger and larger 2D grids:

The selector keeps one index per 16-index block and chooses its final two digits so every selected point has the same type—the terminal state \(I\) in our construction. Because their types match, the Hilbert pair law can relate the height displacement between any two selected lifts to their planar vector displacement. Each selected index then becomes its height:

\[P_a=\bigl(H(n_a),n_a\bigr)\in\mathbb Z^3.\]

One selected point per block makes the walk infinite. Successive selected indices differ by 4 to 28, which bounds both the floor displacement and the height displacement. The walk therefore uses only finitely many 3D steps.

2
Prove the key identity

The Hilbert pair law connects floor displacement to height.

All selected indices finish in the same Hilbert state. For any two of them, \(m<n\), the planar displacement is \(H(n)-H(m)\), while the height displacement is exactly \(n-m\). The pair law equates a fingerprint of the floor vector with the 2-adic valuation of the height gap:

\[ V\bigl(H(n)-H(m)\bigr)=\nu_2(n-m). \]

Here \(\nu_2(k)\) counts the factors of 2 in a nonzero integer \(k\). For a nonzero planar vector \(u=(u_x,u_y)\), let \(p(u)\) be the lower coordinate valuation and let \(\epsilon(u)\) record whether the two valuations tie:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(u)&=\min\{\nu_2(u_x),\nu_2(u_y)\},\\ \epsilon(u)&= \begin{cases} 1,&\nu_2(u_x)=\nu_2(u_y),\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}, \end{cases}\\ V(u)&=2p(u)+\epsilon(u),\qquad \nu_2(0)=\infty. \end{aligned} \]

The first base-4 digit where \(m\) and \(n\) differ determines both sides of the pair law. Scaling a planar vector by \(k\) adds \(2\nu_2(k)\) to \(V\), while scaling the height gap by \(k\) adds only \(\nu_2(k)\) to its valuation. That factor of two is what breaks a supposed straight line. See the pair-law derivation.

3
Rule out a line

Assume three selected points are collinear.

The lift rule makes the three heights \(a<b<c\). Write the adjacent height gaps and floor displacements as

\[ \begin{gathered} A=b-a,\qquad B=c-b,\\ X=H(b)-H(a),\\ Y=H(c)-H(b). \end{gathered} \]

Collinearity makes the two 3D chords proportional. Comparing their height coordinates and clearing the ratio gives

\[BX=AY.\]

Write \(A=gr\) and \(B=gs\), with \(r\) and \(s\) coprime. Integer divisibility then gives one planar vector \(W\) with \(X=rW\) and \(Y=sW\).

Apply the Hilbert pair law to all three pairs

The indices \(a,b,c\) have the same terminal state, so Part 2 applies to each pair. Using \(V(kW)=V(W)+2\nu_2(k)\) gives:

1. Adjacent pair \((a,b)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} V(X)&=\nu_2(A),\\ V(rW)&=\nu_2(gr),\\ V(W)+\nu_2(r)&=\nu_2(g). \end{aligned} \]
2. Adjacent pair \((b,c)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} V(Y)&=\nu_2(B),\\ V(sW)&=\nu_2(gs),\\ V(W)+\nu_2(s)&=\nu_2(g). \end{aligned} \]
3. Endpoint pair \((a,c)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} V(X+Y)&=\nu_2(A+B),\\ V((r+s)W)&=\nu_2(g(r+s)),\\ V(W)+\nu_2(r+s)&=\nu_2(g). \end{aligned} \]

Compare the adjacent pairs. Their last equations give \(\nu_2(r)=\nu_2(s)\). Since \(r\) and \(s\) are coprime, both valuations are zero: \(r\) and \(s\) are odd. Substituting back also gives \(V(W)=\nu_2(g)\).

Now use the endpoint pair. Its last equation and \(V(W)=\nu_2(g)\) give \(\nu_2(r+s)=0\). The endpoint pair therefore says \(r+s\) is odd.

Contradiction

\(r+s\) would have to be both even and odd.

The adjacent pairs make \(r\) and \(s\) odd, so their sum is even. The endpoint pair makes the same sum odd. Therefore the three selected lifts cannot be collinear.
