Research and understanding log

How the robot idea became a proof I could explain

The first Hilbert staircase failed almost immediately. Later, even after the formal argument existed, teaching it exposed places where compact language hid essential distinctions. This timeline keeps both kinds of progress visible.

Why include the moments of confusion?

A polished proof can make its decisive choices look inevitable. They were not. The failures explain why a selector is needed; the learning questions explain why terminal state, fixed lookup, vector fingerprint, and endpoint parity each deserve their own step.

Starting idea

Lift a Hilbert path into 3D

Use the Hilbert route index as height. This turns a flat winding path into a staircase that always rises.

Fast failure

The robot cannot land on every stair

Indices 3, 4, and 5 already form a straight line after lifting. This exact counterexample killed the simplest version.

More failed rules

Keeping corners or every few indices is not enough

Simple periodic choices still left collinear triples. The selector had to use the orientation carried by the recursive Hilbert decoder.

Key discovery

One terminal state carries an exact pair law

For two indices with the same final orientation, the first base-4 mismatch controls both the valuation of their index gap and a fingerprint of their planar chord.

Selector

One fixed representative from every 16-index block

A two-digit base-4 suffix cancels any incoming state. The fixed lookup I→5, S→1, T→13, C→3 gives successive index gaps from 4 through 28.

General contradiction

Odd, odd, and an impossible odd sum

On a supposed line, the adjacent pair laws make two coprime scale factors odd. The endpoint pair law says their sum is odd. But odd plus odd is even.

Independent replay

500,000 steps reconstructed exactly

A separate program recomputed all 500,001 vertices and all 16 realized steps from the construction. It matched the saved artifact exactly.

Exhaustive finite check

Every direction in the finite artifact checked

A separate four-core verifier checked all 125,000,250,000 earlier-point directions. No direction repeated from one vertex, so no three recorded vertices are collinear.

Lean closure

The infinite argument and finite menu are kernel-checked

Lean checks the transducer, pair law, terminal-state selector, no-three-collinear contradiction, Hilbert adjacency, and membership of every step in one fixed finite set.

Teaching pass

The proof was rebuilt from the reader’s questions

The guided lessons separated sixteen suffixes from four states, explained why the selector is a lookup rather than a search, derived the common vector W coordinate by coordinate, and made the endpoint oddness equation explicit.

Current status

Unconditional AI-assisted construction; outside review pending

The Lean theorem constructs the infinite walk and excludes every ordered triple. Independent mathematical review remains the next load-bearing external step.

Who did what

Erik Kalviainen, a math hobbyist and engineer, directed the research through prompts to GPT-5.6 using Pi, Oh My Pi, and Paseo. Erik chose the questions, rejected unclear explanations, asked for exact counterexamples, and steered the project toward reproducible checks and Lean. GPT-5.6 performed much of the algebra, coding, adversarial testing, writing, and formal proof development.

The repository records the successful route, failed approaches, finite artifacts, and formal sources. The guided proof also records which explanations survived a from-memory reconstruction by the person steering the work.
